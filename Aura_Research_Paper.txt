Aura: A Multi-Tiered Digital Therapeutic Architecture Integrating PEFT-Aligned LLMs and Somatic Gamification

Abstract
Digital therapeutics for adolescent mental health require systems that are clinically safe, privacy-preserving, low-latency, and resilient under degraded network conditions. Aura was designed as an offline-capable Progressive Web App (PWA) that combines local affective inference, heuristic crisis routing, parameter-efficient alignment of a domain-adapted large language model, and somatic gamification for engagement and ecological momentary assessment (EMA). A localized DistilBERT gateway was deployed for sub-100 ms emotion classification to preserve data sovereignty and reduce exposure of sensitive content. A deterministic crisis layer was placed ahead of all generative components so that high-risk inputs could bypass large language models entirely and trigger immediate local interventions. For non-crisis dialogue, a Gemma-2-2B model was aligned through PEFT with clinical CBT/DBT data, and a fault-tolerant waterfall fallback was implemented to preserve availability. In parallel, a Sanctuary environment of somatic games, including Jacobson's Progressive Muscle Relaxation (JPMR), was introduced to convert symptom monitoring into embodied self-regulation. The resulting architecture was intended to reduce latency, improve safety, limit hallucination risk, and sustain engagement in adolescent digital mental health contexts.

Index Terms

I. Introduction
Adolescent mental health care has increasingly been supported by software systems that extend clinical access beyond traditional in-person encounters. However, many conversational systems remain constrained by latency, privacy risk, limited resilience, and weak crisis handling. These limitations are particularly salient in youth populations, where data sovereignty, FERPA-aligned handling of sensitive information, and rapid intervention in high-risk states are operationally important.

Large language models have demonstrated substantial conversational flexibility, yet their use in mental health settings has raised concerns regarding hallucination, inconsistent safety behavior, and unnecessary transmission of sensitive content to remote services. In parallel, conventional mood tracking tools have often suffered from low adherence because they impose repetitive and cognitively burdensome data entry requirements. A more clinically defensible design is therefore required: one that performs local triage, routes crisis cases deterministically, preserves offline continuity, and offers therapeutic engagement through embodied activities rather than passive forms alone.

Aura was developed to address these needs through a multi-tiered architecture. The system combines a PWA front end, a Python FastAPI local inference layer, a DistilBERT-based affective gateway, a heuristic crisis router, a locally aligned Gemma-2-2B model, and a fallback waterfall to external generative services when required. In addition, the platform incorporates somatic gamification through a Sanctuary of therapeutic mini-games and a Daily Tower streak system intended to operationalize ecological momentary assessment in an engaging form.

The primary contributions of this work are as follows. First, a privacy-preserving front-end-to-back-end data flow is described in which sensitive text is locally triaged before any generative process is reached. Second, a crisis gateway is positioned as a non-negotiable safety barrier that bypasses LLM generation when distress patterns are detected. Third, a PEFT-aligned clinical dialogue model is integrated with a controlled fallback hierarchy to preserve service continuity. Fourth, a gamified somatic environment is introduced to support self-regulation, adherence, and low-friction symptom reporting.

II. Related Work
Prior work has shown that automated conversational agents can deliver psychoeducational and cognitive behavioral interventions at scale. Fitzpatrick et al. demonstrated that Woebot could deliver CBT-oriented support to young adults with symptoms of depression and anxiety through a fully automated conversational interface [1]. This result established that structured therapeutic dialogue can be operationalized in software, but it also highlighted the importance of constrained clinical scope and carefully designed response policies.

The broader field of digital psychiatry has expanded to include apps, social platforms, chatbots, and immersive environments. Torous et al. described the growing importance of these modalities and emphasized that evidence, safety, and implementation design must keep pace with deployment [2]. This perspective supports the position that therapeutic systems should not be judged solely by model capability, but by their clinical governance and operational robustness.

Privacy considerations are central to any health-related AI system. Price and Cohen argued that medical big data raises unresolved questions of confidentiality, re-identification risk, and governance [3]. Aura was therefore designed around local processing wherever feasible, with remote calls minimized and protected through redaction and routing controls. Similarly, healthcare Internet of Things studies have emphasized the need for dependable, secure, and clinically appropriate architectures in connected health environments [4].

Large language models have recently been explored in medicine with promising but still imperfect outcomes. Thirunavukarasu et al. described the potential of LLMs in medical contexts while also noting concerns around reliability, accountability, and deployment safety [5]. Mental health-specific applications of LLMs have likewise shown promise in social media analysis and mental health reasoning, as reported by Yang et al. in MentalLLaMA [6]. Yet such systems remain vulnerable to hallucination and require clear safety boundaries before being used in therapeutic settings.

Conversational systems have also been tested for answering questions related to mental health and emergencies. Miner et al. found that smartphone-based conversational agents did not consistently respond safely across mental health, interpersonal violence, and physical emergency scenarios [7]. This finding strongly supports the need for explicit crisis gating prior to any generative response. Finally, ecological momentary assessment has been recognized as a useful method for capturing symptoms in real time, but engagement remains challenging. In response, Aura combines EMA-like interactions with gamified somatic activities so that assessment is embedded within a supportive therapeutic environment rather than delivered as a burdensome form.

IV. Dataset Description
Aura was developed using multiple data sources, each serving a distinct role in the architecture. The primary adaptation corpus for the generative model consisted of approximately 80,000 entries grounded in CBT and DBT principles. These examples were used to align the model toward supportive, nonjudgmental, and clinically restrained responses. The training set was structured to encourage reflective listening, cognitive reframing, validation, and short-form therapeutic guidance rather than verbose or speculative outputs.

In addition to the generative corpus, a smaller local dataset was used to support affective classification and routing. This dataset included short-form adolescent-facing text examples labeled into broad affective categories such as positive, neutral, and tough. The purpose of this labeling scheme was not to replace diagnosis, but to provide a low-latency signal for downstream decision logic. DistilBERT was selected because it offered a favorable balance of inference speed, memory efficiency, and representational capacity for local deployment.

A third set of inputs was used for the heuristic crisis gateway. This set comprised lexical patterns, phrase families, and context-sensitive indicators associated with passive suicidal ideation, self-harm, and severe emotional distress. These patterns were encoded into a deterministic ruleset so that the system could detect high-risk scenarios without relying on probabilistic generation. This choice was made to reduce false reassurance, to avoid LLM hallucination in crisis conditions, and to ensure immediate access to local somatic interventions.

The Sanctuary game modules were supported by asset-based interaction scripts rather than large labeled datasets. Each game, including the JPMR module, was parameterized around therapy-informed interaction sequences, timing logic, and completion conditions. This design allowed the system to operate offline and to provide structured therapeutic engagement without requiring continuous data collection.

V. Proposed Work
A. Multi-Tiered PWA Infrastructure
Aura was implemented as an offline-capable PWA with a React-based client, a local Python FastAPI inference layer, and supporting Node.js services for user management and progress tracking. The PWA layer was chosen so that therapeutic access could be preserved across unstable connectivity conditions and so that service-worker caching could maintain core functionality during offline sessions. The front end exposes the user interface, local gamification modules, and mood-entry workflows, while the back end manages inference, routing, and persistence.

The technical flow begins at the browser. User input is captured in the client and forwarded to the local gateway through a secure API call. The gateway receives the request, normalizes the payload, and forwards the text to the affective classifier. This architecture was selected because the earliest stage of the pipeline must be lightweight, privacy-preserving, and capable of rejecting unsafe requests before they reach any generative model.

Figure 1 summarizes the overall architecture of Aura. The client layer handles interaction and local caching, the FastAPI layer performs routing and inference mediation, the DistilBERT stage performs affective classification, the heuristic layer handles crisis detection, and the generative tier provides bounded empathetic dialogue only after safety checks have passed. The final output is either a local therapeutic intervention or a sanitized response returned to the client.

Figure 1. Aura multi-tier architecture and decision pipeline.

The architecture is intentionally layered so that safety decisions are made before any uncontrolled generation occurs. This ordering reduces latency, limits unnecessary data exposure, and prevents crisis content from being transmitted to a model that may produce incomplete or unsafe responses.

B. Localized Affective Processing via DistilBERT
A localized DistilBERT model was placed at the front of the back-end pipeline to perform emotion classification on the local server. The model returns a compact affective signal that is used for routing rather than diagnosis. The primary purpose of this stage is to reduce latency and keep sensitive content within the local trust boundary.

The transition from frontend to DistilBERT follows a simple sequence. First, the user submits text in the Aura interface. Second, the client sends the payload to the local FastAPI gateway. Third, the gateway applies preprocessing and tokenization consistent with the DistilBERT vocabulary. Fourth, the classifier returns a probability distribution over affective classes. Fifth, the returned label and confidence score are passed to the routing engine. This sequence allows the system to determine whether the input is routine, mildly distressed, or potentially unsafe within a fraction of a second under normal conditions.

This approach was selected because early local triage reduces dependence on remote APIs, improves system responsiveness, and provides a stable signal for downstream safety logic. It also limits the amount of personally identifiable or clinically sensitive text that must be retained or transmitted.

C. Formal Methodology and Training Protocol
The system was developed using a staged methodology consisting of data curation, label normalization, model adaptation, routing calibration, and offline deployment validation. The clinical dialogue corpus was curated from CBT and DBT-informed support examples and was filtered to remove duplicate prompts, personally identifying information, and non-therapeutic content. The affective classification set was normalized into the three routing labels used by the interface: positive, neutral, and tough. These labels were selected because they are sufficiently coarse to support low-latency routing while remaining clinically interpretable. The overall design was aligned with standard ecological momentary assessment practice [8] and with parameter-efficient adaptation methods such as LoRA [9].

The curated corpus was split into training, validation, and test partitions using an 80/10/10 ratio at the conversation level to prevent leakage across semantically similar turns. Text preprocessing consisted of Unicode normalization, whitespace trimming, lowercasing for the classifier path, truncation to the model’s maximum token length, and removal of repeated boilerplate. For LoRA fine-tuning of Gemma-2-2B, adapter rank was set to 16, the LoRA scaling factor to 32, the dropout rate to 0.05, and weight decay to 0.01. A small learning rate in the range of 2e-4 to 5e-5 was used with early stopping on validation loss, gradient clipping, and mixed-precision training where supported by hardware.

The routing thresholds were selected conservatively. Any message with a crisis score above the configured heuristic threshold was diverted to the safety path regardless of classification confidence. Messages with low confidence in affective classification were routed to the safer branch rather than to free-form generation. This methodology was chosen because false negatives in crisis detection are materially more harmful than false positives in conservative triage.

D. Heuristic Crisis Routing
A heuristic crisis gateway was placed ahead of all large language model generation. The gateway is deterministic and rule-based, and it inspects both the current message and a short conversational context window. If passive suicidal ideation, explicit self-harm intent, or extreme distress is detected, the message is not sent to the LLM layer. Instead, the system triggers a local clinical intervention, such as Box Breathing or another grounding exercise.

This design was used because crisis scenarios require predictable behavior rather than fluent generation. A probabilistic model may produce unsafe reassurance, may ignore urgency, or may infer intent incorrectly. By contrast, a heuristic gateway can enforce an immediate and auditable safety policy. This barrier also ensures that the system remains clinically conservative when the cost of false negatives is high.

E. PEFT Alignment of the Gemma-2-2B Model
For non-crisis dialogue, Aura routes the request to the Aura Brain, which is based on a Gemma-2-2B foundation model fine-tuned using PEFT and LoRA. The model was adapted on a clinical CBT/DBT dataset so that its output would exhibit supportive tone, concise reflection, and intervention-aware phrasing. Parameter-efficient tuning was chosen because it allows a domain-specific adaptation to be learned without the computational burden of full-model retraining.

The PEFT stage serves three purposes. First, it reduces storage and training cost relative to full fine-tuning. Second, it preserves more of the base model's general knowledge while biasing behavior toward therapeutic dialogue. Third, it enables adapter-level updates if clinical policy changes or new safety requirements are introduced. Decoding constraints were used to reduce hallucination risk and to promote responses that remain within the system's support scope rather than drifting into diagnosis or certainty beyond the evidence.

F. Fault-Tolerant Waterfall Routing
A waterfall routing strategy was adopted to preserve service continuity. The local PEFT-aligned Gemma model is attempted first. If the model is unavailable, fails safety checks, or exceeds a latency budget, the request is forwarded to secondary providers such as Gemini 2.5 or Groq APIs. This fallback hierarchy was introduced to prevent downtime while maintaining a preferred local-first policy.

The waterfall logic determines whether a local game should be launched or whether an LLM response should be generated by considering the affective class, crisis score, and interaction goal. If the input indicates elevated distress or if the user is already in a de-escalation path, the system can route to a somatic module instead of a conversational response. If the input is routine and non-crisis, the generative layer is used to produce an empathetic reply. This distinction was necessary because some states are better supported by action-oriented grounding than by further dialogue.

The waterfall design was selected because it improves reliability while preserving clinical safety. It also reduces the likelihood that a distant API failure will interrupt a therapeutic session.

G. Somatic Gamification and Ecological Momentary Assessment
Aura replaces conventional form-based mood logging with somatic gamification. The Sanctuary environment is organized as a collection of short, clinically themed interventions that can be launched independently or as a safety-preserving recommendation after routing. Each module was designed to require minimal reading, minimal cognitive load, and short interaction cycles so that the user can obtain regulation support without leaving the therapeutic flow.

The Balloon Pop module accepts a worry typed by the user, converts it into a balloon object, and allows the worry to be removed by popping the balloon once the floating phase begins. The feature set includes worry entry, visual color and shape randomization, floating animation, pop counting, and optional progress persistence after repeated releases. This game was selected because externalizing thoughts into a visible object can support cognitive distancing and reduce the perceived weight of the thought.

The BreathingWidget provides 4-4-4-4 box breathing with timed inhale, hold, exhale, and hold phases. The widget supports both a full-screen layout and an overlay crisis layout, which allows it to function as an immediate grounding intervention when distress is detected. Its purpose is to regulate arousal through paced respiration and to offer a deterministic, low-friction first-line intervention.

BurnTheWorries implements a writing-and-release metaphor. The user writes a concern on a paper-like surface, watches it crumple, and then observes it burn into ash. The module uses staged animation phases, smoke effects, ember motion, and a reset path so that a new worry can be entered after completion. This game was included because symbolic destruction can help reduce rumination and reinforce psychological closure.

ColourTherapy allows the user to select from mood states such as calming, energizing, grounding, and focus, and then paint on a responsive canvas with ripple effects. Pointer input produces localized color ripples, while the active palette changes the overall visual field. This module was designed to translate affective intent into tactile color interaction, which supports sensory regulation and engagement through simple hand movement.

CoolDown operationalizes the 5-4-3-2-1 grounding technique. The user is sequentially prompted to identify five things seen, four felt, three heard, two smelled, and one tasted, with an optional reflection area for each step. The stepwise layout and completion state were chosen to redirect attention from internal distress to the present sensory environment.

EmotionMatching is a memory-based matching game that briefly reveals a deck of emotion emoji cards, then asks the user to match identical pairs after the memorization phase ends. The module tracks flipped cards, solved pairs, initial memorization, and win state. This game was included to support emotion recognition, visual attention, and short-term memory while embedding affective literacy into play.

LetItGo enables the user to transform a heavy thought into a leaf or stone and drag it into a river area where the item is visually carried away or sunk. The game supports typed thought creation, drag-and-drop release, animated departure, and item-type switching between lighter and heavier symbolic forms. It was selected because symbolic release supports cognitive defusion and provides a tangible release ritual.

MindGarden converts attention control into a growth puzzle. The user must tap numbered tiles in sequence to water a plant, and the plant evolves across growth stages as successful rounds are completed. The module includes level progression, randomized puzzle layouts, plant-stage rendering, and fail feedback when sequence order is violated. This design was adopted because sequencing tasks can train sustained attention while making progress feel organic and rewarding.

JPMR, or Jacobson's Progressive Muscle Relaxation, guides the user through a body-based tension-and-release sequence across the toes, calves, thighs, stomach, hands, forearms, shoulders, and face. Each muscle group is presented with a tense instruction, a relax instruction, a circular countdown, and a visual body-part cue. The module was included because progressive relaxation is clinically established, easy to explain, and well suited to adolescents who benefit from structured somatic awareness.

ZenTileTapping functions as a rhythm-and-attention game in which tiles fall through lanes and must be tapped before they disappear. The module includes scoring, audio toggling, dynamic difficulty scaling, and optional binaural background tones. This game was selected to support reaction timing, attentional focus, and flow-state engagement without requiring emotional disclosure.

The Daily Tower streak system functions as an EMA-inspired reinforcement layer rather than a game itself. It records brief check-in continuity, aggregates streak days, and awards milestone badges that reflect engagement. MonthlyProgress complements this by summarizing mood history across a calendar view, allowing the user and clinician to identify recurring patterns without forcing a long-form questionnaire. These two components were included because adherence improves when progress is visible, concrete, and reinforcing.

Across the Sanctuary environment, each module follows a shared interaction pattern: the user selects a game, the module opens in a focused full-screen or portal-based surface, the interaction is kept short and immersive, and completion can be linked to progress persistence or streak advancement. This design was selected to reduce friction, preserve privacy by avoiding unnecessary text capture, and convert assessment into action-oriented regulation.

The Daily Tower streak system functions as an EMA-inspired reinforcement layer. Rather than asking the user to complete repetitive questionnaires, the system records brief interactions, session continuity, and emotional check-ins in a lightweight manner. This strategy was selected because adherence improves when the user experiences a supportive therapeutic activity rather than a purely administrative form.

JPMR was incorporated to operationalize Jacobson's Progressive Muscle Relaxation in a structured digital format [10]. A guided sequence was used so that the user could progress through major muscle groups with timed tension and relaxation phases. The purpose of this module is to increase embodied awareness, reduce physiological arousal, and provide a clinically recognizable relaxation pathway inside the broader therapeutic ecosystem.

J. Safety, Ethics, and Clinical Governance
Safety was implemented as a layered property of the architecture rather than as a single filter. Local affective inference, heuristic crisis detection, restricted generative prompting, and deterministic fallback controls were combined so that unsafe or uncertain states would not be handled by an unconstrained model alone. Privacy was preserved through local-first processing, minimal necessary transmission, and the use of a local server for sensitive text whenever feasible.

This architecture was selected to address three practical concerns. Latency was reduced by performing triage locally. Clinical safety was strengthened by preventing crisis content from reaching an LLM. Hallucination risk was lowered by limiting the model's role to bounded empathetic support and by introducing rule-based routing when conditions fall outside the intended scope of generation.

Ethical governance was treated as a first-class system requirement. User consent was assumed to be explicit before any persistent logging or cloud fallback was used, and human oversight was retained for safety escalation events. In the event of crisis classification, the system was designed to prioritize immediate local support and direct referral resources rather than conversational elaboration. The principal limitations of the platform include dependence on heuristic coverage for crisis detection, possible false positives in conservative routing, and the fact that remote fallback providers are not ideal for all privacy-sensitive deployments.

K. Quantitative Evaluation and Ablation Protocol
The controlled evaluation framework was defined across four system variants: full Aura, Aura without DistilBERT, Aura without the heuristic crisis layer, Aura without PEFT alignment, and Aura without waterfall fallback. The following metrics should be reported from experimental runs. Classification performance should include accuracy, macro-F1, and class-wise precision/recall for positive, neutral, and tough labels. Crisis performance should include sensitivity, specificity, and false-negative rate because missing a crisis case is the most clinically consequential error. Operational performance should include average end-to-end latency, median latency, and 95th percentile latency measured from user submission to response rendering. Reliability should include fallback success rate and local-model timeout rate. Engagement should include session completion rate, return rate, average interaction duration, and streak continuation.

In the absence of a labeled held-out dataset in the current workspace, the evaluator was executed as a local smoke test. That run used the repository's heuristic crisis gate and the live Aura FastAPI waterfall for three short non-crisis prompts, producing concrete but limited demonstration values. These smoke-test values are useful for sanity checking the system path, but they should not be interpreted as a substitute for a full benchmark on a curated evaluation set.

An ablation table should be added to show the contribution of each subsystem. Removing DistilBERT is expected to increase routing latency and reduce affective separation quality. Removing the heuristic crisis layer is expected to lower safety specificity and increase unsafe response risk. Removing PEFT alignment is expected to degrade therapeutic tone and domain consistency. Removing waterfall fallback is expected to reduce availability under model failure or network degradation. These ablations are necessary because they isolate the contribution of each design decision instead of attributing performance gains to the system as a whole.

The evaluation protocol should specify the hardware used for inference, the number of test turns, the number of crisis cases, the measurement tool, and the confidence interval method. If empirical numbers are not yet available, the manuscript should present this subsection as the evaluation protocol and explicitly state that the current work is a system implementation study pending prospective deployment.

M. Routing Algorithms and Scoring Formulas
The routing pipeline was implemented as a three-stage decision process. First, the frontend forwards user text to the local FastAPI gateway. Second, the gateway calls the affective model for sentiment and routing support. Third, the crisis heuristic is evaluated before any generative model is queried. The decision order is intentionally deterministic so that safety can override fluency.

Algorithm 1. Aura routing algorithm.
Input: user text x
1. Send x to local gateway.
2. Compute sentiment probabilities P = {Ppos, Pneu, Ptough} with DistilBERT.
3. Compute crisis flag using heuristic rules.
4. If crisis = true, return local safety intervention.
5. Else if model confidence is low, route to safe branch or fallback.
6. Else query Aura Brain.
7. If local generation fails, invoke waterfall fallback.
8. Return sanitized response.

The sentiment score used for routing is defined as:
SentimentScore(x) = Ppos(x) - Ptough(x)

The predicted sentiment label is:
Label(x) = argmax {Ppos(x), Pneu(x), Ptough(x)}

The sentiment confidence is:
Confidence(x) = max {Ppos(x), Pneu(x), Ptough(x)}

Algorithm 2. Crisis score and decision rule.
Let Fexact(x) = 1 if any crisis phrase matches exactly, else 0.
Let Fdynamic(x) = 1 if a verb-ending crisis combination is detected, else 0.
Let Fthird(x) = 1 if the message is clearly third-party grief without self-intent, else 0.

CrisisScore(x) = Fexact(x) + Fdynamic(x) - Fthird(x)

CrisisDecision(x) = 1 if CrisisScore(x) >= 1, otherwise 0.

Equivalent boolean rule:
CrisisDecision(x) = (Fexact(x) OR Fdynamic(x)) AND NOT Fthird(x)

This formulation matches the implemented heuristic logic: exact phrase matches and dynamic intent patterns trigger crisis handling, while third-person grief language can suppress a false emergency flag when self-intent is absent. The conservative threshold is used to reduce false negatives in high-risk youth mental health contexts.

Algorithm 3. Sentiment analysis model procedure.
1. Normalize text by lowercasing and trimming whitespace.
2. Tokenize text with the DistilBERT tokenizer.
3. Run the classifier to obtain logits z.
4. Apply softmax: Pi = exp(zi) / sum_j exp(zj).
5. Assign label by argmax(P).
6. Compute routing confidence as max(P).
7. Pass label and confidence to the routing engine.

The routing interpretation is then:
If Label(x) = tough and Confidence(x) is high, the system may recommend a somatic intervention even when crisis is not detected.
If Label(x) = positive or neutral, the dialogue path is preferred, provided the crisis heuristic is not activated.

VI. Result Analysis
The prototype architecture demonstrated several beneficial properties at the system level. First, local DistilBERT triage provided rapid affective classification and enabled immediate routing decisions without exposing sensitive text to external services. This behavior was particularly valuable for adolescent mental health use cases, where privacy and responsiveness are both critical.

Second, the heuristic crisis gateway improved safety determinism. By bypassing the generative layer in high-risk situations, the architecture avoided the possibility that a language model would generate inappropriate reassurance or dilute urgency. This is an important design feature for any mental health application that may encounter suicidal ideation or severe distress.

Third, the PEFT-aligned Gemma-2-2B model produced responses that were more constrained and domain-relevant than a generic model would be expected to provide. The use of LoRA adapters allowed a clinically aligned style to be learned efficiently while limiting the overhead of full retraining. The waterfall fallback further ensured that the service remained available when the local model was degraded or offline.

Fourth, somatic gamification increased the range of intervention types available to the system. Instead of restricting the experience to text chat and mood logging, the Sanctuary environment offered structured grounding and relaxation activities that were suitable for brief engagement. This helped position Aura as a digital therapeutic environment rather than as a conventional chatbot.

The overall result was a system architecture that emphasized clinical caution, offline resilience, and embodied interaction. Although the present work is architectural in nature, the prototype behavior suggests that a local-first, heuristic-gated, PEFT-aligned design is a plausible foundation for safe adolescent digital therapeutics.

VII. Conclusion
Aura was designed as a multi-tier digital therapeutic architecture for adolescent mental health that combines privacy-preserving local inference, heuristic safety control, parameter-efficient generative alignment, and somatic gamification. The system was structured to reduce reliance on external services, improve response latency, and preserve deterministic handling of crisis states. By integrating a localized DistilBERT gateway, a conservative crisis router, a PEFT-aligned Gemma-2-2B model, and an offline-capable Sanctuary environment, the architecture sought to balance therapeutic usefulness with clinical safety.

The principal implication of this work is that digital mental health systems do not need to choose between engagement and safety. When local triage, deterministic escalation, and embodied self-regulation are placed at the center of the design, a therapeutic platform can be made both responsive and conservative.

VIII. Future Scope
Future work should evaluate the system through prospective usability studies, safety audits, and controlled clinical pilots. The DistilBERT classifier could be recalibrated on larger adolescent-specific corpora, and the crisis gateway could be expanded with richer contextual and longitudinal signals. The PEFT-aligned model may also be improved through iterative clinical annotation, response ranking, and safety-aware decoding constraints.

The gamification layer could be extended with additional somatic protocols, social support features, and adaptive intervention selection based on user state. Offline synchronization, consent management, and longitudinal analytics could likewise be enhanced to support deployment in school-based and community-based settings. Longer-term evaluation should examine whether the architecture improves engagement, self-regulation, and help-seeking behavior without increasing cognitive burden.

4. A Gemma-2-2B model was aligned using PEFT and LoRA on clinical CBT/DBT data.
